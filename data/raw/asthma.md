# Asthma

## Overview
Asthma is a chronic inflammatory disease of the airways. The airways become swollen and narrow and produce extra mucus, making breathing difficult. Symptoms vary over time and in intensity, and attacks can be triggered by allergens, infections, exercise or air pollution.

## Symptoms
Typical symptoms are wheezing, shortness of breath, chest tightness and coughing, often worse at night or early in the morning. Symptoms that come and go and are triggered by exercise, cold air or allergens suggest asthma.

## Triggers
Common triggers include house dust mites, pollen, mould, pet dander, cigarette smoke, air pollution, cold air, respiratory infections such as colds, strong smells and some medicines such as aspirin and other NSAIDs in sensitive people. Identifying and avoiding personal triggers reduces attacks.

## Diagnosis
Diagnosis is based on symptom history and lung function tests. Spirometry measures how much and how fast a person can breathe out; an improvement after inhaling a bronchodilator supports the diagnosis. Peak flow monitoring at home helps track variability.

## Treatment
Treatment uses inhalers. Reliever inhalers contain fast-acting bronchodilators such as salbutamol to open the airways quickly. Controller (preventer) inhalers contain inhaled corticosteroids to reduce airway inflammation and are taken regularly. Current GINA recommendations favour an inhaled corticosteroid-formoterol inhaler as a reliever for adults and adolescents instead of salbutamol alone. Correct inhaler technique, often with a spacer, is essential.

## Asthma Attack
Signs of a severe asthma attack include breathlessness that makes it hard to speak, rapid breathing, blue lips, and no improvement after using a reliever inhaler. Sit upright, take the reliever inhaler as instructed in the asthma action plan and call emergency services if symptoms do not improve.
