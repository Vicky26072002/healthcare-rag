# Type 2 Diabetes

## Overview
Type 2 diabetes is a chronic condition in which the body does not use insulin properly (insulin resistance) and, over time, the pancreas cannot make enough insulin to keep blood glucose in a normal range. It is the most common form of diabetes and is strongly linked to excess body weight, physical inactivity and family history.

## Symptoms
Many people have no symptoms for years. When symptoms occur they can include increased thirst, frequent urination, increased hunger, unexplained weight loss, tiredness, blurred vision, slow-healing sores and frequent infections. Tingling or numbness in the hands or feet can be a sign of nerve damage.

## Risk Factors
Risk factors include being overweight or obese, age over 35 to 45 years, a family history of diabetes, physical inactivity, a history of gestational diabetes, prediabetes, high blood pressure and abnormal cholesterol levels. People of South Asian origin tend to develop type 2 diabetes at a lower body weight and younger age.

## Diagnosis
Diabetes is usually diagnosed with blood tests. Common criteria are a fasting plasma glucose of 126 mg/dL or higher, an HbA1c of 6.5% or higher, or a 2-hour glucose of 200 mg/dL or higher during an oral glucose tolerance test. An HbA1c between 5.7% and 6.4% indicates prediabetes. Results are generally confirmed with a repeat test.

## Management
Lifestyle changes are the foundation of treatment: a balanced diet with controlled portions of refined carbohydrates, at least 150 minutes of moderate physical activity per week, weight loss when overweight, and stopping smoking. Metformin is commonly the first medicine prescribed. Other medicines include SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, DPP-4 inhibitors, sulfonylureas and insulin. The choice depends on the person's other conditions and must be made by a doctor.

## Complications
Poorly controlled diabetes damages blood vessels and nerves. Complications include heart attack and stroke, kidney disease (diabetic nephropathy), eye damage (diabetic retinopathy), nerve damage (neuropathy) and foot ulcers that may lead to amputation. Regular eye exams, kidney function tests and foot checks help detect problems early.

## Hypoglycemia
Low blood sugar (hypoglycemia) can happen with insulin or sulfonylureas. Symptoms include shakiness, sweating, confusion, fast heartbeat and hunger. A person who is awake can take 15 grams of fast-acting sugar such as glucose tablets or fruit juice and recheck after 15 minutes. Severe hypoglycemia with unconsciousness is an emergency.
